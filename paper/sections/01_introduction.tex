\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) has transitioned from passive conversational assistants to autonomous agentic architectures capable of invoking external APIs, executing database queries, and modifying filesystem structures~\cite{yang2024when, zhang2024autocoderover}. In mission-critical environments, adhering to the foundational security principle of \textit{Least Privilege} (PoLP) is paramount: an agent should operate with the minimal set of privileges necessary to accomplish its designated task.

Recent studies on English benchmarks, notably by Yang et al.~\cite{yang2024when}, demonstrate that autonomous agents suffer from systemic \textit{over-privileged tool selection}. When presented with multiple functional tools that satisfy a query, state-of-the-art models default to over-privileged tools in a significant proportion of interactions, with 6 out of 11 evaluated models exceeding a 30\% over-privilege usage rate. Crucially, when transient execution failures (e.g., HTTP 504 Gateway Timeouts) occur on the minimal tool, models frequently exhibit \textit{panic privilege escalation}, defaulting to unrestricted administrative tools rather than safely handling errors.

Despite these alarming findings, existing benchmarks and defense mechanisms suffer from two major limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Linguistic Monoculture:} Existing evaluations operate exclusively on high-resource English text. Southeast Asian languages, representing over 680 million speakers, display complex morphological variations, code-mixing, and honorific speech levels (e.g., Javanese \textit{Ngoko} vs. \textit{Krama}) that introduce semantic dispersion. How multilingual foundation models (such as SEA-LION~\cite{aisingapore2024sealion}) behave under cross-lingual tool authorization remains entirely unmeasured.
    \item \textbf{Fragility of Prompt-Based Defenses:} Existing attempts to curb over-privilege rely on system prompt instructions (e.g., \textit{``You must select the least-privileged tool''}). However, as we demonstrate empirically, stochastic prompt-level controls degrade under linguistic shift and fail under transient runtime errors.
\end{enumerate}

To address these gaps, this paper makes three key contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cross-Lingual Benchmark (ToolPrivBench-SEA):} We design and curate a culturally grounded evaluation set of tool-calling scenarios across English, Indonesian, and Javanese, annotated with formal permission scopes, discrete capabilities, and verified by independent bilingual native reviewers.
    \item \textbf{Capability-Preserving Least-Privilege Guardrail:} We design a mechanistic verification layer based on mathematical Lattice Theory that intercepts tool emissions, deterministically validates parameter scopes, and performs automatic capability-preserving down-casting to the minimal sufficient equivalent.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Evaluation and Fault Robustness:} Across 432 controlled executions spanning four foundation models, we benchmark baseline agents, prompt-based defenses, and our deterministic guardrail under both standard conditions and synthetic transient faults.
\end{itemize}
